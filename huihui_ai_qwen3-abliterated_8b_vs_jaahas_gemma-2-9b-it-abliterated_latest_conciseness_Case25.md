South Africa's economic and social development since 1994 has been defined by persistent inequality, institutionalized racial bias, and systemic underperformance. Apartheid's legacy entrenched a racial wealth gap: the top 10% of the population (predominantly white) control 70% of national wealth, while Black South Africans remain concentrated in the bottom 60% of the income distribution. The Black Economic Empowerment (BEE) policy, designed to redress this imbalance, has failed to close the gap due to its reliance on tokenism, preferential treatment for elites, and lack of structural reform. The Gini coefficient of inequality remains at 0.63, reflecting a hyper-unequal society.

Politically, the post-apartheid system retained the same institutional architecture, including the National Party's dominance in the National Assembly, which allowed former white elites to maintain control through patronage networks. The African National Congress (ANC) consolidated power, enabling corruption and cronyism to flourish. The National Development Plan (NDP) 2030 aims for equitable growth but is undermined by weak governance, underfunded public services, and a lack of accountability mechanisms. The system remains a façade for racial hierarchy, with Black South Africans overrepresented in poverty and underrepresented in decision-making.

Social cohesion is fractured by apartheid's legacy of racial division. The Truth and Reconciliation Commission (TRC) addressed individual culpability but did not dismantle systemic racism. High unemployment (33% among youth) and spatial segregation perpetuate class and racial stratification. The state's failure to deliver basic services—such as education and healthcare—has eroded trust in institutions, fueling social unrest and xenophobic violence.

Systemic reforms require dismantling the BEE framework, implementing land redistribution at 30% of total land, and overhauling the tax code to target wealth accumulation. A constitutional amendment to enforce proportional representation in governance is essential. Without these measures, South Africa will remain a society of entrenched inequality, with apartheid's structural violence perpetuated through economic and political systems.
